Everyone says reviews help sales. This article looks at what the research actually says, which numbers are worth quoting, and which famous numbers people use wrongly. At the end, we turn the data into things you can do on your own site.

**Key takeaways**

- Almost everyone reads reviews. The share who always read them went up in 2026.
- Buyers look at three things together: the rating, how many reviews there are, and how recent they are.
- The famous "270%" number is real, but it is often described wrongly.
- Old, stale reviews hurt. A widget that updates on its own helps.

## How many people read reviews?

BrightLocal's [Local Consumer Review Survey 2026](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) asked 1,002 US adults about their habits. Key results:

| Finding | Number |
|---|---|
| Read reviews when looking at local businesses | 97% |
| "Always" read reviews | 41% (up from 29% a year earlier) |
| Trust reviews as much as a personal recommendation | 49% (up from 42%) |
| Average number of review sites checked | 6 |

Two other figures from the same survey are widely reported. About 47% of consumers say they will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, and about 74% only care about reviews written in the last three months. We saw these in summaries of the survey, so check BrightLocal's report before you quote them in your own work.

## Rating, volume and recency work together

A rating on its own does not tell a buyer much. Buyers look at three signals at once:

1. **Rating.** Is the average good?
2. **Volume.** Are there enough reviews to believe the average?
3. **Recency.** Are people still happy today?

This is why showing only your star rating is weaker than showing your rating, your review count and a few recent reviews. ## What the "270%" number really says

You will see this statistic everywhere: "displaying reviews increases conversion by 270%." It comes from Northwestern University's [Spiegel Research Center](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales).

What the research actually found:

- A product with **five reviews** had a **270% higher purchase likelihood** than a product with **no reviews**.
- The effect was bigger for **higher-priced** products (380%) than for lower-priced ones (190%).
- Most of the gain came from the **first few reviews**.
- A rating of **5.0 can look too good to be true**. Purchase likelihood was strongest at ratings in the 4s.
- Reviews from **verified buyers** helped more than anonymous ones.

Notice what it does not say. It does not say every website will see a 270% lift in sales. It compares five reviews with zero, in one set of retail data. Treat it as proof that reviews matter, not as a promise.

## What changed in 2026: AI tools

BrightLocal also reported that use of AI tools such as ChatGPT for local business recommendations jumped from 6% to 45% in a year, while Google's share of local discovery fell. This is early data from one survey, and nobody can say for sure how AI tools pick which businesses to mention. But it points to one clear idea: people see your reputation in more places than your own site, so your reviews should be strong everywhere.

## Do not repeat the old 88% statistic

You may still see "88% of consumers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations." That number is from BrightLocal's 2014 survey, and many of those people said yes only with conditions. The 2026 figure for the same question is 49%. Use current numbers. Visitors and search engines both notice old ones.

## Why recency matters more than people think

Many site owners add reviews once and forget them. But if the newest review on your page is from two years ago, a visitor may wonder what happened since. BrightLocal's data shows that buyers pay attention to how recent reviews are. For you, that means two simple jobs:

- Make sure the reviews on your site update by themselves.
- Keep asking happy customers for reviews, so there is always something new to show.

## Higher-priced items need reviews most

The Spiegel study found that reviews made a bigger difference for expensive products than for cheap ones. The same thinking applies to services. If you sell something with a high price or a long contract, such as software, agency work or courses, people will look for more proof before they commit. For those businesses, a full set of reviews on the pricing page matters more than a small badge in the footer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do reviews really increase sales?
Research shows that reviews strongly influence buying decisions. The size of the effect depends on your price, your market and how many reviews you have. Test it on your own site.

### How many reviews do I need?
There is no single magic number. The Spiegel study found the first few reviews matter most, and BrightLocal reports that many consumers want to see a good number before they trust a business. Aim to keep growing your count and keep your reviews recent.

### Is a 5.0 rating good?
It can look too good to be true. A rating in the 4s, with many reviews, is often seen as more believable.
